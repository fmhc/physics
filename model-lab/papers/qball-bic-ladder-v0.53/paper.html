Abstract

We study localized linear excitations of a classical complex scalar Q-ball in three spatial dimensions with potential 𝒰(S)=S−S2+S3/2\mathcal U(S)=S-S^2+S^3/2. In a regime with one propagating and one evanescent exterior channel, we assemble computer-assisted existence arguments for two radial modes and one dipole mode on the infinite radial domain. Analytic exterior estimates and ball-arithmetic interior enclosures distinguish these results from finite-box linewidth minima. A numerical radial sequence, with candidates reported through index eighteen, motivates a thin-wall phase-matching description. An independently computed planar transmission zero yields an unfitted spacing candidate; identifying this value with a radial limiting spacing remains open. A second-harmonic analysis separates one dipole monopole outgoing coefficient from zero under explicitly audited exact-mode and enclosure hypotheses. It obstructs a specified uniformly localized smooth continuation for every linked dipole polarization with 𝐩T𝐩≠0\boldsymbol p^T\boldsymbol p\ne0; circular polarization requires a separate quadrupole certificate. Linear existence, numerical spacing and nonlinear continuation are treated as distinct statements.

A non-technical overview

This section explains the setting and the main statements without technical prerequisites. Figure 1 summarizes it in one picture. The precise statements, their evidence and their limits are those of the later sections; nothing here adds a result.

What is a Q-ball?

A Q-ball is a lump of a field that holds itself together. The field in this paper is a complex scalar field, a quantity with a size and an internal phase at every point of space. In a Q-ball the size forms a round, localized profile, while the internal phase turns steadily in time, like the hand of a clock with angular frequency ω\omega. The density of the field, and therefore the shape of the lump, does not change in time (Fig. 1a). The rotation carries a conserved charge QQ, which plays the role of a particle number. Coleman showed that, in suitable theories with such a conserved charge, lumps of sufficiently large charge have less energy than the same charge carried by free particles; such lumps cannot simply fall apart and are stable [1]. This general argument concerns large charge; the stability of the particular backgrounds of this paper is not established here. Friedberg, Lee and Sirlin had earlier constructed related nontopological solitons, which also rely on a conserved additive quantum number, in a model with an additional neutral field [30]. Q-balls with a large charge have a nearly uniform interior and a transition zone of fixed thickness at the surface [1]. In the present model this thin-wall regime is reached as ω2\omega^2 approaches 1/21/2; the wall keeps its thickness while the radius grows, so the wall becomes thin relative to the ball.

What holds it together?

The shape follows from a balance of competing tendencies. Gradients cost energy, so the field profile is smoothed and any edge is softened. Far outside, the field decays to zero because of its mass and because ω2<1\omega^2<1. The potential 𝒰(S)=S−S2+S3/2\mathcal U(S)=S-S^2+S^3/2 of the field density S=|ϕ|2S=|\phi|^2 contains a negative term −S2-S^2, which lowers the energy relative to free, non-interacting field, and a positive term S3/2S^3/2, which penalizes high density. At fixed conserved charge this combination permits binding; in the thin-wall regime the preferred interior density tends to S=1S=1. Coleman gave an intuitive picture of this balance [1]: the equation for the radial profile is the equation of motion of a particle whose position is the field value and whose time is the radius. The particle moves in a landscape with two hills and is slowed by a damping term that becomes weaker at large radius (Fig. 1b). A Q-ball corresponds to a particle that starts at rest on the inner side of the outer hill and comes to rest exactly on the hill at zero field. For the present potential this is possible only for 1/2<ω2<11/2<\omega^2<1. Close to ω2=1/2\omega^2=1/2 the two hills have almost equal height; the particle then starts very close to the top and lingers there, which gives a large uniform core with a comparatively thin wall.

Vibrations, two channels and radiation.

A Q-ball can vibrate. A small vibration with frequency ρ\rho of the rotating lump appears in the field at two partner frequencies, ω+ρ\omega+\rho and ω−ρ\omega-\rho; these coupled sidebands are the standard description of Q-ball perturbations [2]. Far from the ball, a component can travel outwards as a wave only if the magnitude of its frequency exceeds one, the mass of the field in our units; below that threshold its tail decays exponentially. A negative frequency, as for ω−ρ\omega-\rho here, only means that the phase turns the other way. In the regime studied here one partner lies above the threshold and the other below it (Fig. 1c). The upper partner is an open channel: it can carry energy away to infinity as radiation. The lower partner is a closed channel: it is trapped near the ball. Excited Q-balls have vibrational modes [2], and in a one-dimensional version of the same type of model a squashed Q-ball relaxes by radiating through such channels [3]. A typical vibration in our regime should therefore leak energy and fade. Its leakage rate is measured by the width of the resonance: the larger the width, the faster the vibration fades.

When the radiation vanishes.

How much energy leaks depends on the amplitude of the outgoing wave in the open channel. Contributions to this wave from different parts of the ball can cancel each other by interference. When they cancel completely, the width is zero and the vibration stays exactly localized although a radiation channel is open. Such a state is a bound state in the continuum (BIC): a localized wave that coexists with extended waves of the same frequency but does not radiate [27]. The idea was proposed in quantum mechanics as early as 1929 by von Neumann and Wigner, and BICs have since been studied in many wave systems, particularly in photonics; one known route is the cancellation of radiating components, for example when two interacting resonances interfere so that the width of one of them vanishes [27, 4]. Figure 1d shows the computed mode C0: its open-channel component decays like the trapped one instead of leaving an outgoing tail.

Why this is not automatic.

Three reasons make such cancellations nontrivial. First, once the background and the overall size of the vibration are fixed, the outgoing wave must vanish in both of its oscillation phases. These are two real matching conditions for the two frequencies ω\omega and ρ\rho. Generically this suggests that cancellations, if they occur, sit at isolated parameter points that have to be found. The proofs below establish existence inside small parameter boxes; they do not establish that the parameter pair is unique. Second, for a single wave scattered by an object of finite extent, such trapped states are generally excluded [27]. The Q-ball problem is of a different kind: the vibration has two coupled components, one of which cannot propagate. For the linearization about the ground state of the focusing cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation in three dimensions, embedded eigenvalues are proved to be absent [34]; the present relativistic model is different, and nothing here contradicts that theorem. Third, a computer calculation in a finite box can show a very small width that is not exactly zero. Only a proof that controls the infinite exterior separates an exact zero from a near miss. This is the reason for the computer-assisted proofs in Section 4. They use interval arithmetic, which carries guaranteed bounds through every rounded arithmetic operation, together with analytic estimates that bound the remaining approximation errors and the infinite exterior.

Several things are not shown. There is no proof of an infinite ladder, and the identification of the flat-wall value with the actual limiting step remains open; a frozen formula for the absolute locations failed its test. The stability of the Q-ball backgrounds themselves is not established. A numerical check of how the charge changes with the frequency bears, under the assumptions of a standard criterion, only on one simple kind of instability, a purely growing radial deformation, and finds no sign of it (Section 8). Finally, the cancellation is a statement about small, linear vibrations. At finite amplitude, nonlinear terms can drive second harmonics, with laboratory frequencies ω±2ρ\omega\pm2\rho; for the certified modes these lie in the propagating range, but this alone does not establish outgoing radiation. For the dipole mode C2, under stated hypotheses, one outgoing second-harmonic coefficient is guaranteed to be nonzero. This obstructs a specified smooth localized continuation to finite amplitude, while circular polarization needs a separate certificate (Section 7). For C1 the corresponding calculation is open. The linear trapping found here therefore does not establish a nonlinear trapping, and no lifetime or instability is inferred.

Potential significance.

The following points are possibilities for further work, not claims of this paper. Numerical simulations indicate that Q-balls can form in the early universe through the fragmentation of an Affleck–Dine scalar condensate [6], and supersymmetric Q-balls have been proposed as a contribution to dark matter [7]. Whether comparable linear cancellations occur in such models, and whether they matter for how long internal vibrations last, are open questions; the present model, its parameters and the nonlinear limitation noted above support no statement about lifetimes or observations. In photonics, BICs underlie sharp resonances with high quality factors; in real structures of finite size they appear as quasi-BICs, weakly leaking approximations to an ideal BIC [19], with applications ranging from lasers and sensors to filters [27]. The Q-ball zeros share the interference and resonator logic of such systems; this is an analogy of mechanism, and no experimental realization is proposed. Finally, the combination of interval arithmetic with analytic control of the infinite exterior used here is a general method: it can separate exact embedded eigenvalues from small numerical widths in other soliton problems, in the spirit of related computer-assisted spectral work [35, 25]. None of this amounts to a stability result, a particle model or an experimental proposal.

Question and scope

A small resonance width, an exactly localized linear mode and a localized finite-amplitude oscillation require different evidence. We examine this separation for a complex scalar Q-ball, reporting three existence enclosures, a broader numerical sequence and a wall-scattering interpretation of its spacing. The sequence label nn is a continuation index, not a certified node count or a completeness statement. A bound state in the continuum (BIC) denotes a localized linear mode in an available propagating-channel regime [27]. Uncertified locations will be called candidates. All quantities are dimensionless.

Relation to Q-ball excitations and continuum trapping

Q-balls are localized scalar configurations carrying a conserved global charge [1]. Battye and Sutcliffe study collisions, charge transfer and fission in one, two and three spatial dimensions in the same sextic model, after unit conversion [14]. Kovtun, Nugaev, and Shkerin formulate the coupled sideband problem ω±ρ\omega\pm\rho and analyze three-dimensional vibrational modes [2]. Azatov, Ho, and Khalil treat scattering in the sextic family, as well as the complex-plus-real-field Friedberg–Lee–Sirlin model [8, 30]. The coupled radial operator is established prior work; the contribution here concerns particular localized solutions of it, with regular origin conditions and controlled infinite tails.

Chen, Andersson, and Li analyze three-dimensional Q-ball perturbations for the rescaled potential V(s)=s2−s3+κs4V(s)=s^2-s^3+\kappa s^4, where ss denotes the field modulus [32]. Their coupled-sideband classification provides context, but concerns a different potential. Our claim is the certification of three particular localized linear modes of the sextic model, including cancellation of both oscillatory open-channel components. Among the works reviewed here, we did not identify a predecessor for these specific modes. This limited statement establishes neither global priority nor a contradiction with the spectrum studied by Chen and collaborators.

Zhang, Zhou, and Zhu obtain analytic scattering solutions from stepwise Q-ball profiles [9]. Their interior dispersion and thin-wall phase structure are directly relevant to Eq. (16), although their propagating scattering regime has two exterior open channels. Ciurla et al. distinguish bound, half-propagating and quasinormal modes in 1+11+1 dimensions [3]. Their half-propagating interval 1−ω<ρ<1+ω1-\omega<\rho<1+\omega has the same channel thresholds as ours, but their long-lived example retains a small radiative tail. Evslin et al. analyze paired Floquet modes of small-amplitude, one-dimensional Q-balls [11]; coupling of a discrete component to its propagating partner produces Feshbach-type quasinormal modes. These one-dimensional calculations do not transfer the spherical origin condition or radial measure to three dimensions.

Numerically assisted spectral exclusion arguments for NLS solitary waves include those of Marzuola and Simpson and Asad and Simpson [25, 26]. Cuccagna and Maeda impose absence of embedded eigenvalues as hypothesis H4 and describe the broader ground-state expectation as unproved in their 2020 survey [33]. Li and Yang subsequently prove absence in the essential spectrum for the full linearized operator about the ground state of the three-dimensional focusing cubic NLS [34]. This is a different equation and operator from our relativistic Q-ball frequency pencil. A nodeless Q-ball profile alone does not establish the relevant variational ground-state class. The present model-specific existence claim therefore does not contradict that NLS theorem or refute the survey’s expectation in its stated setting.

A bound state in the continuum (BIC) is a spatially localized linear state at a frequency also admitting extended radiation modes [27]. Interference-induced trapping is an established mechanism: Friedrich and Wintgen relate interacting resonances to vanishing decay width [4], and Yu and Lu give an existence theorem for a three-channel Schrödinger problem with one open channel [5]. Its small-coupling assumptions and finite-range potential differ from the present two-channel pencil with an infinite profile tail. Related resonant reflection occurs for discrete breathers, optical solitons and condensate domain walls [20, 21, 22].

Winding numbers for BICs are also established: Zhen et al. use the winding of a real far-field polarization vector in the two-dimensional Bloch wave-vector plane of a photonic slab [36]. Their symmetry assumptions support a charge-conservation and robustness statement. Our winding of a matching map over Q-ball parameters is a numerical zero-finding diagnostic; it does not inherit that photonic robustness theorem.

The planar transmission zero and interior phase matching admit a Fabry–Pérot interpretation. Hsu et al. describe BICs formed by perfect resonant reflection together with a round-trip phase condition, including a resonator paired with a reflecting boundary [27]. In the thin-wall picture used here, the reflecting wall and the regular centre supply the analogous radial cavity. This is an interpretation of the wall and phase calculations, rather than an additional existence result for the curved Q-ball. In particular, the planar zero alone does not certify a spherical mode. Sequences of radiation cancellations and WKB matching are also known for nonlinear embedded solitons [12]; their model and scaling parameter differ from those of the present sequence of linear modes on changing backgrounds.

Nonlinear radiation obstructions have a separate history. Segur and Kruskal find beyond-all-orders radiation excluding small-amplitude true breathers in the one-dimensional ϕ4\phi^4 model [28]. Sigal studies instability of periodic and quasiperiodic wave solutions under generic nonlinear perturbations [29]; Soffer and Weinstein prove radiation damping under a Fermi-golden-rule hypothesis [13]. Manton and Merabet derive second-harmonic radiation with amplitude of order A2A^2 and power of order A4A^4 from a kink’s internal mode in 1+11+1-dimensional ϕ4\phi^4 theory [37]. The harmonic mechanism and power counting are known; their coefficients and perturbative decay law are not transferred to our three-dimensional Q-ball. The present contribution in this direction is the explicit error budget separating a particular second-harmonic coefficient from zero. Its continuation obstruction has the stated hypotheses and is not a dynamical decay theorem. Oscillon decay minima [10] and the second-harmonic radiation zeros of a critical-bubble internal mode [24] further illustrate why suppression at one harmonic does not establish an exactly localized nonlinear oscillation.

Lührmann et al. combine analytic estimates and interval arithmetic to certify vortex profiles, spectral data and nonzero Fermi-golden-rule coefficients in the self-dual abelian Yang–Mills–Higgs model in two spatial dimensions [35]. Their internal mode is a gap eigenvalue. This is a methodological precedent for rigorous spectral and radiation-coupling computations. Here the distinct target is a particular outgoing second-harmonic coefficient associated with a localized linear Q-ball mode in three dimensions. Its conditional continuation obstruction remains separate from a nonlinear decay, lifetime or background-stability theorem.

Background stability is likewise distinct from mode localization. Classical stability results for charged scalar solitons and the Vakhitov–Kolokolov slope criterion provide the appropriate context [30, 31]. Applying such a criterion requires its model-dependent hypotheses and charge convention; the certificates here do not determine dQ/dωdQ/d\omega at the three backgrounds. A numerical evaluation and its limited scope are given in Section 8. The literature comparisons delimit the claimed contribution without establishing exhaustive priority or an infinite ladder.

Earlier radial evidence

At C0, finite-amplitude radial evolutions support fourth-order scaling of a nonlinear-remainder flux and an outgoing second harmonic. Its spline inputs and free exterior are fixed data, so it does not certify that overlap for the exact continuum mode. Supplement Section S2.2 retains these numerical values and limitations.

Discussion and conclusion

The strongest result is the existence of three localized linear modes with analytic treatment of the infinite exterior. The remaining radial locations have the numerical evidence classes recorded in Table 2. The unfitted planar spacing candidate provides a mechanism to test; a controlled radial curvature limit and an infinite-ladder theorem remain open and are stated as Conjecture 1. The failed absolute-location test in Section 6 constrains the finite-order approximation.

The conditional monopole enclosure obstructs uniformly localized smooth continuation for the linked dipole polarizations with 𝐩T𝐩≠0\boldsymbol p^T\boldsymbol p\ne0. A complete quadrupole certificate is still required for circular polarization. The numerical power table has a different normalization and is not recertified by this bound. An operator realization of the full evolution generator would strengthen the spectral interpretation.

Limitations and open problems

Nonlinear questions. Three nonlinear questions remain open. First, the second-harmonic obstruction for C2 rests on an enclosed L=0L=0 coefficient and therefore covers only polarizations with 𝐩T𝐩≠0\boldsymbol p^T\boldsymbol p\ne0; circular polarization couples only to L=2L=2. Approximate-input (floating-point) diagnostics of the L=2L=2 channel, including the numerical response of Supplement Section S2.1, motivate a quadrupole certificate but provide neither a bound nor a proof; circular polarization remains open. Second, no second-harmonic coefficient is enclosed for the radial modes: the C0 diagnostic of Supplement Section S2.2 does not bind the exact mode, and the forced problem for C1 has not been solved. Third, no lifetime is derived. An outgoing power of order ϵ4\epsilon^4 in the mode amplitude ϵ\epsilon of Section 7.1 determines a decay law only together with the energy of the linear mode, including its sign, and a controlled reduction to a slow amplitude equation; neither is provided here.

Computational trust base.
